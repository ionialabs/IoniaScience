Imagine a future where ultra-secure quantum information zips across continents in minutes, enabling a global quantum internet. Achieving this vision requires advanced quantum memories that can store and synchronize quantum bits — or qubits — with high accuracy and speed. Recently, scientists have demonstrated a quantum memory that excels simultaneously in three critical performance areas: storing multiple quantum modes, operating with over 80% efficiency, and maintaining fidelity above 99%. This breakthrough brings us closer to scalable quantum interconnects capable of distributing quantum information reliably over 1000 kilometers.

> **TL;DR**
> - The team introduced a new benchmarking metric called the quantum interconnect rate to evaluate quantum memories comprehensively.
> - They experimentally realized a quantum memory storing 11 spatial modes with uniform efficiency above 80% and qubit fidelity exceeding 99%, enabling high-dimensional quantum information storage.

*Note: this summary is based on a preprint — research shared ahead of formal peer review.*

Quantum communication relies on single photons as carriers of quantum information. These photons can encode data in various ways — such as polarization or spatial patterns — and travel long distances with minimal loss. However, photon loss in optical fibers limits how far quantum information can travel directly. To overcome this, quantum repeaters equipped with quantum memories are essential. Quantum memories temporarily store photonic qubits, synchronizing their arrival for entanglement swapping, which extends communication distances. Moreover, memories capable of storing multiple modes simultaneously can boost communication rates and channel capacity, crucial for building a practical quantum internet.

The researchers used two cold atomic ensembles of rubidium-85 atoms trapped in magneto-optical traps. Single photons were generated and encoded into spatial modes — specifically, high-order Laguerre–Gaussian and perfect optical vortex modes — using spatial light modulators. These photons were stored in the second atomic ensemble via electromagnetically induced transparency, a technique that maps photonic states onto collective atomic excitations. After a controlled storage time, the photons were retrieved and decoded to assess storage efficiency and fidelity. The team characterized 11 spatial modes, achieving uniform high efficiency and fidelity across them.

The quantum memory demonstrated a uniform storage efficiency exceeding 80% across 11 spatial modes, with qubit storage fidelities above 99%. This performance allows reliable storage and retrieval of high-dimensional quantum states (qudits). Using their new quantum interconnect rate metric, the authors estimate that their system could distribute approximately 3.56 bits of quantum information over a 1000-kilometer quantum repeater link within one minute. This represents a significant improvement over previous quantum memory implementations and suggests a practical path toward scalable quantum networks.

This work addresses a key challenge in quantum communication: simultaneously optimizing multiple crucial memory parameters—multimode capacity, efficiency, and fidelity. By introducing the quantum interconnect rate as a holistic benchmark and experimentally achieving high performance across these metrics, the study provides a realistic blueprint for quantum memories that can support long-distance quantum internet infrastructure. The ability to store and manipulate high-dimensional quantum states with high fidelity and efficiency is essential for increasing the data capacity and robustness of future quantum networks.

While the demonstrated quantum memory shows impressive performance, the experiments assume ideal conditions such as perfect detection efficiency and negligible channel decoherence. Real-world deployment will require addressing additional challenges like photon losses in fibers, environmental noise, and integration with other quantum network components. Furthermore, the current storage lifetime of around 28 microseconds limits the maximum distance without further improvements or additional repeater nodes. Continued research is needed to extend memory lifetimes, scale up mode capacities, and integrate these memories into fully operational quantum networks.
