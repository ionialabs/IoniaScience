What happens when light changes the electronic structure of a transparent semiconductor? Scientists have found that shining ultraviolet (UV) light on nickel oxide (NiO) can create a long-lived, metastable electronic state that alters how electrons interact within the material. This light-induced state enhances the mixing—or hybridization—of atomic orbitals, which plays a crucial role in how charges move through the material. Understanding and controlling such states could lead to improved solar energy and photoelectrochemical technologies.

> **TL;DR**
> - UV light excitation of NiO produces a metastable electronic state with enhanced Ni 3d–O 2p orbital hybridization lasting hundreds of picoseconds.
> - This state can be stabilized under continuous low-intensity UV illumination without significant heating, suggesting a non-thermal, light-driven mechanism to tune electronic properties dynamically.

*Note: this summary is based on a preprint — research shared ahead of formal peer review.*

Nickel oxide (NiO) is a well-studied transparent semiconductor widely used as a hole-transport layer in solar cells. Its electronic properties are governed by the interaction between nickel 3d orbitals and oxygen 2p orbitals. Traditionally, tuning these properties has involved static methods like chemical doping, which can introduce defects and disorder. The question arises: can light be used to dynamically and reversibly control these electronic interactions in NiO under operating conditions? This study explores that possibility by investigating how UV light affects the electronic and structural states of NiO.

The researchers employed Ni K-edge X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS), a technique sensitive to changes in the unoccupied electronic states and local atomic structure around nickel atoms. They studied NiO crystals under two types of UV excitation: pulsed laser excitation producing high carrier densities (~10^20 cm^-3) and continuous low-intensity UV irradiation generating much lower carrier densities (~10^13 cm^-3). Complementary first-principles density functional theory (DFT) calculations incorporating electron correlation effects (DFT+U+V) helped interpret the spectral changes in terms of orbital hybridization and electronic screening.

Under pulsed UV excitation, NiO exhibited a metastable electronic state lasting approximately 600 picoseconds, characterized by enhanced hybridization between Ni 3d and O 2p orbitals. This state coexisted with lattice heating and structural disorder. Remarkably, continuous low-intensity UV irradiation stabilized a similar electronic state without measurable lattice heating, indicating that the effect is not solely due to thermal processes. The XAS spectra revealed a red shift near the Ni absorption edge, attributed to stronger orbital hybridization and dynamic screening of electron-electron repulsion. Theoretical calculations supported this interpretation, showing that photoexcitation redistributes charge density and modifies the unoccupied Ni 4p states probed by XAS.

This work demonstrates that light can be used to dynamically and reversibly tune the electronic structure of correlated oxides like NiO without introducing defects or relying on chemical modifications. Since orbital hybridization directly influences charge transport and energy transfer processes, controlling it with light opens new pathways for optimizing materials in solar cells, photocatalysis, and photoelectrochemical devices. The discovery of a long-lived, light-stabilized electronic state suggests novel design principles for developing optoelectronic materials with enhanced performance and tunability.

While the study provides compelling evidence for a light-induced metastable electronic state in NiO, the lifetime of this state under pulsed excitation is on the order of hundreds of picoseconds, which may limit immediate practical applications. The continuous irradiation experiments suggest longer-term stabilization, but further research is needed to understand the stability and scalability of this effect in device contexts. Additionally, the complex interplay of electronic correlations and lattice dynamics requires more detailed investigation to fully harness this phenomenon for technological use.
