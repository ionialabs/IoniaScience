What if reality isn’t a fixed external world but rather a sequence of informational states experienced from a first-person perspective? A new theoretical framework called Algorithmic Idealism invites us to rethink the very nature of existence, consciousness, and the physical universe. This approach, grounded in algorithmic information theory, proposes that what we call reality emerges from patterns of information transitions rather than from an objective world “out there.”

> **TL;DR**
> - Algorithmic Idealism models reality as a series of self-states—informational snapshots of subjective experience—governed by universal algorithmic rules rather than traditional physical laws.
> - This framework offers fresh insights into longstanding puzzles in quantum mechanics, cosmology, and metaphysics, such as the measurement problem, the Boltzmann brain paradox, and the simulation hypothesis.

*Note: this summary is based on a preprint — research shared ahead of formal peer review.*

Traditional physics asks, “What is the case in the world?” Algorithmic Idealism shifts this question to, “What should an agent expect to experience next?” This subtle but profound change focuses on the first-person perspective instead of an external objective reality. The idea is motivated by challenges in quantum theory—like the difficulty of combining observations from different agents—and cosmological puzzles involving observer self-location and identity. It also addresses philosophical problems about personal identity in scenarios like teleportation and computer simulations.

At its core, Algorithmic Idealism treats momentary self-states as fundamental units of reality. These self-states are abstract informational patterns that do not presuppose an embedding in space or time. Transitions from one self-state to another follow a universal induction principle known as Solomonoff induction, which assigns objective likelihoods to possible future experiences based on algorithmic probability. A concrete realization of this idea, called the bit model, represents self-states as finite binary strings, with future states predicted by universal Turing machine computations. This approach rigorously demonstrates that, under certain assumptions, an effective external world can emerge as a stable, computable probabilistic process from these information transitions.

The bit model shows that an observer’s data can asymptotically behave as if embedded in an external computable world, although this world is emergent rather than fundamental. Algorithmic Idealism also provides a new viewpoint on quantum mechanics: it is not an interpretation of quantum theory but a separate framework motivated by quantum puzzles. While it does not identify algorithmic probabilities with quantum Born probabilities by definition, it suggests that in regimes where quantum predictions hold, the first-person probabilities should align with Born-rule statistics. Moreover, the framework offers a novel response to the Boltzmann brain problem by arguing that numerical abundance of such fluctuated observers does not dominate the first-person continuation probabilities. It also reframes the simulation hypothesis by emphasizing that the number of physical or computational realizations of a self-state does not alone determine the likelihood of future experiences.

Algorithmic Idealism challenges our deepest assumptions about reality by proposing that existence is fundamentally informational and experiential rather than physical and external. This shift could unify conceptual issues across quantum physics, cosmology, and philosophy of mind, providing a mathematically rigorous yet philosophically innovative foundation. It invites us to reconsider identity, consciousness, and ethics in a world where reality emerges from algorithmic transitions of self-states, potentially influencing future research on the nature of consciousness and the interpretation of physical theories.

Despite its mathematical rigor, Algorithmic Idealism remains a speculative and conceptual framework without direct empirical validation. Its strongest results apply to specific formal models like the bit model, and broader claims about quantum phenomena, cosmology, and personal identity require further qualification. The framework does not solve the quantum measurement problem outright but reframes it from a first-person perspective. Additionally, the emergent external world it predicts is limited by assumptions such as no information erasure, which the theory’s author is actively working to relax. As such, Algorithmic Idealism is best viewed as an active research program offering promising ideas rather than a definitive theory of reality.
