Did you know that DNA isn’t always the familiar double helix? In certain regions rich in the nucleotide guanine, DNA can fold into complex four-stranded shapes called G-quadruplexes, or G4s. These mysterious knots are found across all forms of life and are emerging as important players in controlling genes, protecting chromosome ends, and maintaining genome stability. Yet, despite decades of research, scientists are still uncovering exactly when and how these structures form inside cells, and what roles they truly play.

> **TL;DR**
> - G-quadruplexes are dynamic four-stranded DNA structures formed in guanine-rich sequences that regulate gene expression and genome maintenance.
> - While G4s are linked to transcriptional control and telomere stability, fundamental questions remain about their formation mechanisms in cells and their diverse biological functions.

Since their discovery in the late 1980s, G-quadruplexes have fascinated researchers as an alternative DNA conformation distinct from the classic double helix. These structures arise when four guanine bases form a planar square called a G-tetrad, and multiple tetrads stack to create a stable four-stranded knot. Although thousands of potential G4-forming sequences exist in the human genome, only a small fraction fold into G4s at any given time inside cells. G4s are often found in gene promoters, telomeres, and other genomic regions critical for regulating DNA replication, transcription, and chromosome integrity. Their dynamic nature and association with important cellular processes suggest they are more than molecular curiosities—they may be key regulators of genome function.

Researchers have used a combination of computational predictions, genome-wide mapping, and specialized antibodies that recognize G4 structures to identify where G4s form in living cells. In vitro studies using purified DNA and biophysical tools like optical tweezers have helped characterize G4 folding dynamics and stability. Additionally, scientists employ small molecules called G4 ligands that selectively bind and stabilize G4s to probe their biological effects. Advances in single-molecule techniques and structural biology are now enabling more precise investigations of how DNA sequence, chromatin environment, transcriptional activity, and G4-binding proteins influence G4 formation and resolution in complex cellular contexts.

Current evidence shows that G4 formation in cells is closely linked to processes that transiently expose single-stranded DNA, such as transcription and DNA replication. For example, the unwinding of DNA by RNA polymerase during transcription creates a ‘bubble’ of single-stranded DNA where G4s can fold. Similarly, replication forks generate single-stranded regions that promote G4 formation. G4s often co-occur with R-loops—structures where RNA hybridizes with one DNA strand, displacing the other—which can stabilize G4s. Proteins including helicases and transcription factors can bind G4s, modulating their stability and function. Functionally, G4s can act as hubs recruiting transcriptional regulators, sometimes enhancing gene expression, or in other cases repressing it. At telomeres, G4s help regulate telomerase access and chromosome end protection. Importantly, dysregulation of G4 formation or resolution is linked to genome instability, replication stress, and diseases such as cancer and neurodegeneration.

Understanding G-quadruplex biology holds promise for advancing our knowledge of gene regulation and genome maintenance mechanisms. Because G4s are involved in key cellular processes and their misregulation is associated with disease, they represent potential targets for therapeutic intervention. For instance, drugs that stabilize G4s can selectively impair cancer cell replication or induce telomere shortening. Moreover, insights into how G4s form and function could reveal new layers of epigenetic regulation and genome organization. As research tools improve, the study of G4s may open new frontiers in molecular biology, offering fresh perspectives on the dynamic architecture of the genome.

Despite significant progress, many fundamental questions about G-quadruplexes remain unanswered. It is still unclear which predicted G4 sequences actually fold into stable structures in living cells at specific times and how cells regulate this dynamic equilibrium. The precise molecular mechanisms by which G4s influence transcription and other processes are incompletely understood, partly because experimentally separating the effects of G4 folding from DNA sequence changes is challenging. Additionally, the interplay between G4s, chromatin context, transcriptional activity, and G4-binding proteins is complex and context-dependent. Future studies combining advanced single-molecule approaches, genome editing, and high-resolution imaging will be needed to fully elucidate the biological roles of G4s and their potential as therapeutic targets.
